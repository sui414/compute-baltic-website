Compute Margin — what a chip prints, and what's left

The full pricing chain for a unit of compute: buy the silicon, feed it tokens, sell the tokens at the settled market price. Physics from open benchmarks, prices from the routing layer, rents from the cross-venue composite.

ABOUT · The whole pricing chain for a unit of compute, end to end: buy the silicon (CapEx), feed it tokens, sell the tokens at the settled market price. This tab joins three datasets: SemiAnalysis InferenceX open benchmarks (tokens/sec per GPU at every interactivity level — the physics), OpenRouter settled token prices from our daily scrape (token-weighted across providers — the market), and this desk's own rental composite (the OpEx alternative). Every dollar figure is per single GPU (one chip) — for NVL72 racks that means one of its 72, with the rack’s fabric priced into both its cost and its throughput. The ledger’s “As sold” column shows the whole-system number a purchase order is actually written for.

Assumptions to keep honest: the ceiling columns assume the GPU is sold out 24/7 at the chosen operating point — the ledger’s break-even utilization column inverts that assumption, and the realization waterfall below prices the whole haircut chain; token prices are OpenRouter token-weighted effective rates averaged over the trailing 7 days (the same window the rents use), and are already net of cache discounts; revenue is per installed GPU, with disaggregated prefill/decode configs rescaled onto the full pool (unrescaled, the rack rows read about 3× high); rents are on-demand hourly throughout (reserved, spot and interruptible products are collected but excluded — they price a different promise and sit below on-demand), and are venue asks and posted rate cards with one exception — SF Compute is an order book and prints cleared trades, and it clears H100 about 30% below this composite, so the quoted cost leg reads high and the token margin low (the revenue leg is biased the other way, being a sold-out ceiling — the two errors oppose); power + colo excluded (≈$0.10–0.25/GPU-hr); purchase prices are street-quote ranges that move monthly (B300 early list quotes currently sit below B200 street — scarcity pricing, not physics). Read this as a map of relative economics between chips, not a business plan. The cost → tokens → revenue → margin framing of this tab owes to Marvin Tong (Phala Network).

Margin History · top prints on B200

The margin is a curve, not a constant — the same chip’s gross margin, model by model, marked daily.

sources: openrouter settled effective prices (daily) · inferencex throughput (config held at latest benchmark) · cross-venue B200 rental composite

Gross margin = (ceiling − rent) ÷ ceiling, per model on one B200, at each day’s OpenRouter token-weighted effective prices against the day’s cross-venue B200 rental median. Throughput is held at the latest benchmark config, so every move you see is the market repricing tokens or rent — not the silicon changing.

HOW TO READ · Two cost bases, two questions. Rent is the price of machine time — no tokens involved. Token margin answers the renter’s question: rent the chip by the hour, sell tokens off it — what’s left? (hourly prints − hourly rent; the one-time cost never enters it). Break-even answers the owner’s question: buy the machine outright — how many months of token revenue repay the buy-in? Prints = tokens generated per hour × OpenRouter settled price per token (output + input legs), at ≥30 tok/s per user, sold out 24/7. Hover any header for the formula and any Buy-in cell for that row’s price source (street-quote ranges — Mercatus, IntuitionLabs, Tom’s Hardware, TechPowerUp — mid-2026, cross-checked against the live secondary-market table above).

Secondary Market Resale Value

What the chip sells for used — the market's own depreciation curve.

Sources: Compute Exchange hardware market — used / refurbished, daily snapshot
HOW TO READ · The other exit: don't run it, sell it. Compute Exchange (the DRW-backed GPU procurement marketplace) posts indicative ranges and supply levels for used and refurbished data-center GPUs — the empirical depreciation curve to weigh against the break-even clock. A used H100 SXM at $22–27k against ~$31–40k deployed-new says the chip has kept most of its value three years in; V100s at $1–2k say where every vintage eventually goes.

Contract Tiers · the same chip, three different products

Every H100 price we hold on one axis, grouped by what you are actually buying.

Sources: SF Compute cleared order book · vast.ai on-demand and bid books · RunPod secure/community/spot/reserved ladder · Prime Intellect on-demand and spot · Mithril · ORNN · Silicon Data · SemiAnalysis 1-yr contract index · neocloud and serving-platform rate cards
HOW TO READ · Colour is the contract tier; the marker is how the number came to exist — ● cleared trade, ○ posted ask, ◐ index. Compare paired venues, not the whole ladder: a cross-venue gap mostly measures position in the value chain, not term.

On-demand over spot holds wherever one venue posts both — vast.ai $2.40 guaranteed vs $0.90 interruptible (−62%), Prime Intellect $2.35 vs $1.76. Reserved does not: RunPod's posted 3- and 6-month rates equal its secure on-demand rate to the cent, so the published term ladder carries no discount — the real one is negotiated, and a rate card is where it will never show. Note also that within-tier spread beats between-tier spread: on-demand alone spans $1.96–$7.00. For a single GPU-hour, where you buy dominates what term you buy — and the only cleared price in the picture is the cheapest number on it.

Token Margin vs Rental Rate

The operator's spread: printing tokens versus subletting the same chip.

Sources: desk rental composite (vast.ai · ORNN · RunPod · Silicon Data · Prime Intellect · SF Compute) · InferenceX × OpenRouter
HOW TO READ · The crack spread of compute: what a GPU earns selling tokens (median model, chat-grade) minus what the open market charges to rent that same GPU (this desk's cross-source composite where we have it, curated cloud quotes where we don't). Positive premium says the token layer is where the gross profit pools; negative says you'd earn more subletting the chip than running this menu of open models on it. Read H200's ~zero premium as competitive pricing in mature silicon — the median model clears at rent parity while the best model still clears well above it.

Same-Chip Revenue Dispersion

How much the workload changes what one GPU-hour is worth — the nonfungibility measure.

Sources: SemiAnalysis InferenceX · OpenRouter settled prices
HOW TO READ · A barrel of Brent is a barrel of Brent; a GPU-hour is not a GPU-hour. Each line spans the revenue range across every model benchmarked on that chip (chat-grade operating point, log scale) — tick = median. The range is a spread in theoretical gross revenue across the observed model/configuration sample — coverage, precision and tuning depth differ per chip — but even so, a B300-hour serving the right MoE ceilings ~40× above the same hour on the wrong dense model. This spread — plus interconnect (NVL72 racks out-earning their own chips sold loose), memory class, and location/contract terms — is why compute clears as a curve, not a single price.
