The Jevons Test

Inference got 74.5% cheaper. The market got bigger anyway — but not for everyone, and not always for the reason it looks like.

2026-07-17 → 2026-11-21 · 125 days · 515 models · built 2026-11-24

William Stanley Jevons noticed in 1865 that more efficient steam engines did not reduce Britain's coal consumption. They increased it: cheaper work meant more work bought. The same question hangs over inference today, and it now has enough data to answer properly. On OpenRouter, over 125 days, the price of a token fell -74.5% and the number of tokens bought rose 493%. The dollar market did not shrink. It grew 42.7%.

The three series that matter

Price, quantity and spend, all on one basis and all rebased to 100. Price is a unit value — total dollars paid divided by total tokens bought, which is how ORNN and Silicon Data build theirs — quantity is the tokens that actually moved, and spend is their product. Spend is not a separate measurement; it is price times quantity by construction.

How strong is the effect, statistically?

The clean test is a log-log regression of volume on price. The slope is the price elasticity of demand: below −1 means a 1% price cut buys more than 1% of extra volume, so revenue rises. Market-wide the slope is β = -1.32 with R² = 0.788 and t = -21.4 across 125 daily observations — comfortably past p<0.001. Inference demand is elastic, and the fit is tight enough that price explains most of the movement.

Dropping Tencent — the single largest contributor to the volume number — moves the market slope from −1.32 to −1.31 and R² from 0.788 to 0.768. The conclusion does not rest on any one lab.

It does not hold for everyone

The aggregate hides a wide spread. Four patterns show up, and only one of them is Jevons.

1 · Textbook Jevons

DeepSeek is the cleanest case in the dataset: β = −1.39 on an R² of 0.87, price down 73%, volume up 831%, spend up 150%. Z.ai and Meta look the same way. These are labs whose price cuts were met with more than proportional demand, exactly as the paradox predicts.

2 · Growth that is not a price response at all

Tencent posts the largest volume increase in the table by an order of magnitude — +5,216% — and it is the weakest evidence for Jevons in the whole dataset. Its slope is β = −0.36 on an R² of 0.02, which is statistically indistinguishable from no relationship. Price and volume simply are not moving together.

The model-level data says why. Tencent's entire footprint until mid-November was Hy3 and Hy4 preview, together about 1–3T tokens a day. Hy4 flash crossed a billion tokens for the first time on 2026-11-16 and peaked at 23.8T/day within a week. The volume did not come from a cheaper token; it came from shipping a model people wanted. A naive reading of the headline number would file the most dramatic growth story in the dataset as the strongest proof of a price effect, when it is the weakest.

3 · Elastic, but not enough

Anthropic is the counter-example that matters commercially. Price fell 58.8%, volume rose 131.5%, and spend on this venue still fell 4.5%. Its regression slope is β = −1.41, which reads as elastic — but a 59% cut needs roughly 2.4× the volume to stay whole, and 2.3× is what it got. MiniMax is below the line for the same reason.

4 · Prices that went up

Google, StepFun and Xiaomi raised their realised price over the window, so elasticity is undefined for them. Google's spend still rose 133% — demand growth with no price cut at all, which is its own kind of answer.

What this does not prove

The price here is a unit value, and that cuts both ways. It falls when a lab cuts prices, and it also falls when that lab's own buyers rotate onto its cheaper models. Holding the token mix fixed at the July basket, the same posted prices are $0.4234 → $0.4779, up, while the unit value went $0.5577 → $0.1023, down. Most of the "price fall" is migration, not repricing. Some of what reads as elastic demand is substitution inside a lab's own line-up.
This is OpenRouter traffic only. A lab that sells mostly direct can show falling revenue in this channel while its total revenue rises. Anthropic's −4.5% is a statement about this venue, not about the company.
And one day is always missing. The usage panel is upserted during the day, so the newest date is a partial stub and is dropped everywhere rather than charted as a cliff.

So: has Jevons happened?

In aggregate, yes, and with a tight enough fit to say so plainly: inference demand on OpenRouter is price-elastic at β ≈ −1.3, and the dollar market grew 42.7% while the unit price fell 74.5%. Cheaper tokens have made the market bigger, not smaller.

But the per-lab table is the more useful object. It separates labs that bought growth with price (DeepSeek, Z.ai) from labs that bought it with a product (Tencent), from labs that cut price and still lost revenue on the channel (Anthropic). Those are three different businesses, and the aggregate number describes none of them.
